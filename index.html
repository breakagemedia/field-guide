Part 1 · Check any money claim in ten minutes

Four questions. Ten minutes. No advice, by design. Most money claims that go wrong are not invented. They are real numbers that drifted: the date fell off, a projection became a fact. Four questions catch nearly all of it.

1. Who benefits from this number being repeated? Set your effort accordingly. A figure with no institution behind it, repeated in the same phrasing on pages that are selling something, is marketing wearing a statistic's clothes.

2. Is this a primary source, or someone describing one? Primary is the thing itself: the statute, the filing, the regulator's database, the survey with its method attached. Click back. If you cannot reach a primary source in two clicks, that is information about the claim.

3. Are your six sources actually one? One newsroom reports; five outlets carry it. Same quotes, same figures, same names in the same order, and "as first reported by" in small type near the bottom. Checking takes thirty seconds.

4. Is it verified, modelled, or unverified?

LabelWhat it meansHow to treat it
VerifiedYou retrieved it from the source and opened the link yourselfSay it. Cite it.
ModelledYou calculated it, and the method is statedSay it with the method, never as a bare fact
UnverifiedYou have not checked itDo not repeat it. Not even with "I think"

A link you did not open counts as unverified.

The routine

  1. Minute 1. Who benefits? Decide how hard to look.
  2. Minutes 2 to 5. Click back to a statute, a filing, a dataset, or a study with a method.
  3. Minutes 6 to 8. Check whether your several sources are one source.
  4. Minute 9. Check the date, and that the number measures what the sentence says it measures.
  5. Minute 10. Write the label next to the number.

One worked example, in one clause. "Airlines make more from frequent-flyer miles than from flying planes" rests on real June 2020 figures, taken at the bottom of aviation's worst year. By 2024 it had reversed. Add the date and the sentence is true again.

Why the denominator matters: "nearly half the sellers were Black" means nothing alone. Against a county that is 5.4 percent Black (Census, ACS 2023 five-year estimate), it is the whole story. Same numerator.

Part 3 · Twelve words you were never taught

Each entry carries its number and its label; the sources edition (link at the foot) carries the citation.

  • breakage · Value you paid for and never redeemed, which becomes the seller's revenue. About $23 billion a year in US gift cards; 43 percent of adults hold a dead card, averaging $244. VERIFIED
  • escheat · The process by which unclaimed property passes to the state after a dormancy period. Why the unclaimed-property databases exist. VERIFIED
  • deferred interest · Interest accrues from day one and is waived only if the whole balance clears by the deadline. The regulator's example: $4,500 purchase, $180 left owing, about $1,440 charged. About one in five balances trip it. VERIFIED
  • partition action · Any co-owner can force the sale of the whole property, however small their share. VERIFIED
  • heirs' property · Land inherited without a will: everybody owns a share of the whole and nobody owns a specific piece. What makes a partition action possible. VERIFIED
  • guardian ad litem · The person a court appoints for owners who cannot be found. When the search is not diligent, the sale proceeds without them. VERIFIED as reported
  • payment for order flow · Your broker is paid by the firm that executes your trade. The SEC found one broker's customers were "deprived of $34.1 million" in worse prices even after saving the commission; $65 million penalty, settled without admitting or denying. VERIFIED
  • unearned premium · Premium collected for coverage not yet delivered. One insurer, Progressive, held $25.2 billion of it at the end of 2025. VERIFIED
  • float · Money held between receiving it and paying it out. Berkshire Hathaway: about $176 billion at the end of 2025, at a negative average cost in each of the last three years. VERIFIED
  • negative amortization · The payment is smaller than the interest, so the balance grows while you pay on time. $30,000 at 6 percent accrues $150 a month; pay $100 and you owe $30,050. MODELLED
  • churn · Business built on the assumption you will not leave. The FTC's 2022 case against an internet phone company: sign up online, cancel only by phone. $100 million in refunds to 389,106 people. VERIFIED
  • loyalty carve-out · Gift cards get five-year protection under federal rules. A "loyalty, award, or promotional" card is excluded from it, and only has to say so on the front. VERIFIED
